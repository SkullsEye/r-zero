Seismology · Ogata 1988

1. Fraud is a branching process

Every confirmed fraud makes more fraud nearby, soon, and then the effect fades. That is the same object as an earthquake aftershock sequence, and it has a number: the branching ratio.

Simulate a cascade
0total events from one seed
0generations before it died
containedoutcome

Set it below 1 and a burst dies out on its own. Set it above 1 and it never stops. Card fraud sits at 0.438 on IEEE-CIS and 0.574 on an unrelated European card dataset, so bursts are real but self-limiting, which is exactly the regime a defensive model should assume.

We fitted it by maximum likelihood on real transaction times rather than picking it. Shuffling those times destroys the clustering and drives the estimate to zero: over 50 bootstrap resamples the shuffled data never exceeded 0.043.

# the intensity of new fraud at time t λ(t) = μ + Σ α·exp(−β(t − tᵢ)) over frauds already confirmed R₀ = α / β # events caused per event half-life = ln2 / β

The core trick

2. Exponential decay makes it O(1)

Because the kernel is exponential, the running total needs one multiply and one add per event. No window, no history buffer, no nightly batch. That is the whole reason this can run live.

Live contagion on one identity
0.0001-hour half-life
0.0001-day
0.0007-day
0operations used, total

Three clocks run at once, with half-lives of an hour, a day and a week, because a card-testing burst and a slow mule ring keep completely different time. The fast clock spikes and forgets; the slow one remembers a week.

Watch the operation counter. It goes up by two per event no matter how long the identity's history is, which is why 70,000 transactions a second is possible in plain Python. A sliding window over the same history would cost you the window.

# the entire update, per identity, per event elapsed = now − last_seen total *= exp(−rate × elapsed) # one multiply total += weight # one add last_seen = now

Fraudar · Hooi et al. 2016

3. Discount the crowd

An attribute shared by a hundred thousand people carries almost no identity. Weighting every fraud by how common its identity is stops gmail.com from drowning out the signal.

How much is one fraud worth on this identity?
0.000weight of one confirmed fraud
—real example from the data

Without this, the biggest email domain wins every time simply because more fraud passes through it. In our data gmail.com is shared by 172,786 transactions, so one fraud on it moves the needle by 0.082. A device seen three times gets 0.481 — nearly six times as much — because it is actually identifying.

The log is doing the work: it is aggressive on small counts, then almost flat, so a merchant with two million transactions and one with five million are treated as equally anonymous, which they are.

w(identity) = 1 / log(degree + 5) # gmail.com 172,786 seen → 0.082 # a device 3 seen → 0.481

The shape of an attack

4. The identity graph is what fraud travels along

A transaction is not a row. It is a point where a card, a billing address, an email domain and a device meet, and every one of those is an edge to somebody else's transaction.

Hover or tap an identity to see what it touches
the ringtwenty cards, one device, one address, ten minutes

Look at any single payment in that ring and it is unremarkable: a small amount, a card with no history, nothing to flag. Look at the device and the whole thing is obvious. A model that scores rows one at a time can never make that jump, no matter how many trees it has.

We build nine identity families in the full system — card, billing address, client, full card fingerprint, device, device and OS, both email domains, and screen resolution — and the browser model here uses the four that anyone can supply.

ENTITY_KEYS = { "card1", "addr1", "client", "card_full", "device", "device_os", "pemail", "remail", "screen", }

The hard constraint

5. A fraud is invisible until it is confirmed

Nobody knows a transaction was fraudulent at the moment it happens. The chargeback comes back days later. Every number on this site respects that delay.

Drag the delay and watch what the model can see
0.401PR-AUC at this delay
—what it means

This is the single easiest place to cheat, and the easiest to catch. If you let the model see today's outcomes today, PR-AUC jumps to 0.652 and the whole thing is a laboratory result. We hold it at seven days, which is what a real chargeback feed gives you, and report 0.401.

The curve degrades gracefully rather than falling off a cliff, and even at a 45-day delay it is still ahead of having no contagion at all. That matters because it means the system is useful before the feedback loop is fast.

contagion(entity, t) = Σ w(entity)·exp(−β(t − t_fraud)) over frauds where t_fraud + 7 days < t

Wald 1943 · and why we deleted it

6. The damage is a clock, not a threshold

We spent a long time trying to calibrate a sequential test into behaving. It never did, and the reason turned out to be that we were tuning the wrong dial entirely.

How long before a human looks at the decision?
0.234%of innocent customers' payments blocked
5worst case, one customer
21.3%fraud caught — unchanged either way

Wald's sequential test promises a 1% false-alarm rate. Ours delivered 10.64%, so we calibrated it on held-out data and got it to 2.14%. Then we noticed 1,172 of those false alarms came from one innocent account, and no amount of calibration touched it.

Eight attempts later — CUSUM, adaptive conformal control, precision-locked integrators, even a test martingale with Ville's inequality — every single one failed on the same handful of customers. The diagnosis: a per-decision guarantee applied 1,175 times is a per-customer guarantee of 99.996%. The maths was never violated. It was read as though it applied once.

So the threshold was the wrong dial. What actually bounds the damage is how long a wrong decision is allowed to stand. Put a human in the loop and one mistake costs a handful of payments instead of 1,172 — and, as the readout shows, it costs nothing at all in fraud caught.

# not a calibrated constant worst case = review_latency × transactions_per_day # 4 hours × 2,786/day = 465 possible # measured worst on held-out data: 5

Gradient boosting

7. 225 trees, and you can walk one

The features are the idea; the model on top is deliberately ordinary. Here is one real tree from the shipped model, deciding one real transaction.

A real tree, a real transaction, the real path
0.00this tree's contribution
0.00all 225 trees, as a probability

Every node is one comparison. Walk down, land on a leaf, add its value, repeat 225 times, squash through a sigmoid. That is the entire scoring path, and it is why the same model runs identically in Python and in twelve lines of JavaScript.

We check that claim rather than asserting it: the browser reproduces the offline LightGBM scores to 2.22e-16 on all 83,571 transactions, which is machine precision.

while "leaf" not in node: node = node.left if x[node.f] <= node.t else node.right total += node.value

NSGA-II · Deb et al. 2002

8. There is no single best policy, only a frontier

Blocking more fraud always means stopping more innocent customers. Rather than pick a threshold, we hand the business the whole trade-off curve and let them stand where they want.

Every point is a policy that nothing else beats on both axes
₹58.5Mnet saved
1,700customers wrongly blocked
63%of fraud value stopped

An honest negative result lives here. We expected the genetic algorithm to find better policies than random search, and at an equal budget it did not — random search matched it. What NSGA-II actually earns is coverage: 60 evenly spread operating points instead of 6, so the curve you are dragging along is smooth rather than lumpy.

We kept that failure in the README rather than deleting the section, because if every claim in a project holds up, the project was not tested hard enough.

objective(policy) → [ −net_saved, n_false_blocks, ] # minimise both, keep the front

The metric that matters

9. Accuracy is the wrong scoreboard

Call everything legitimate and you are 96.6% accurate. What a fraud team actually experiences is how many of today's hundred reviews were worth opening.

One day's review queue, 100 slots
real fraudinnocent customer
89of 100 were worth opening
1 h 2 manalyst time spent on nothing

The Merchant Risk Council puts a manual fraud review at 5.6 minutes. A hundred reviews is nine hours and twenty minutes, which is more than one analyst's entire day. So the difference between a queue that is 9% real and one that is 89% real is not a decimal point on a metric — it is eight and a half hours of somebody's Monday.

This is why we report PR-AUC and precision at a fixed queue depth, never accuracy, and why the cost model prices a wrongly stopped customer rather than counting errors.

precision@100 = fraud in the top 100 / 100 # standard model 9 / 100 # stacked 89 / 100
